# Some Things Are Time Series, Some Things Are Thresholds

**Subtitle:** We panic over single points in a series and drift into thresholds as if they were just more data.

I have been thinking about a distinction that now seems embarrassingly obvious, but I did not have it clearly named before: some things in life are time series, and some are one-off threshold decisions, and a lot of bad judgment comes from confusing the two. Courtship is a time series. Marriage is a threshold decision. Married life is a time series again. A child’s grades across a semester are a time series. One exam may be a one-off event, but usually it only matters as part of the larger series. A dental implant, by contrast, is much closer to an acceptance problem: do I accept this result, push for rework, or reject it? It is not the same kind of judgment as watching a process unfold over time. Once I started looking at things through that lens, it became clear that we use the wrong cognitive posture all the time. We panic over one point in a series as if the whole process had declared its essence, or we drift into major commitments as if they were just another datapoint instead of a threshold that creates lock-in and starts a new regime.

What follows is not just a terminological distinction. The evaluative framework itself has to change. A time series is not judged the same way as a one-off threshold. With a time series, the point is not to overread one point in the sequence, but to understand the behavior of the process. The framework I ended up with for time series is this:

* **Level**: How good is the average state right now? This is the overall baseline, not the best moment or the worst moment.
* **Trend**: Is the process improving, flat, or degrading? Direction matters at least as much as current position.
* **Variance**: How erratic is the process? A decent average can still hide a system that is too unstable to trust.
* **Shock**: Has there been a serious disruption that changes the interpretation of the series? Not every bad point is a shock, but some events compress time and reveal structure quickly.
* **Recovery**: After stress or error, does the process recover well? Recovery often tells you more than calm periods do.
* **Persistence**: Has the observed pattern lasted long enough to trust your interpretation? A pattern that has not persisted may still be noise.

That is the right way to think about courtship, school performance, health trajectories, employee performance, business traction, product quality, almost anything that unfolds across time. The important thing about a time-series framework is that it changes what counts as evidence. A single bad point is often weak evidence. Five bad points in a row, a downward trend, a failure to recover after a shock, or persistently high variance are much stronger evidence. The whole point is to stop asking whether this moment feels bad and ask instead what kind of process this actually is.

A one-off threshold demands a different frame. Here the question is not whether a process is healthy, but whether this specific outcome should be accepted, delayed, reworked, or rejected. The framework I ended up with for one-off thresholds is this:

* **Target fit**: How close is the actual outcome to what I really wanted? Not abstract perfection, the actual target.
* **Salience**: How noticeable will the deviation be in normal use or ordinary life? Some flaws are real but practically invisible.
* **Functional adequacy**: Does it work as needed, without friction, pain, or serious compromise? Function usually matters more than cosmetic annoyance.
* **Rework cost**: How easy is it to correct later? Some mistakes are cheap to fix, others are expensive, painful, or politically difficult.
* **Irreversibility**: How much future optionality remains if I proceed? Some threshold decisions close off better paths later.
* **Downstream consequence**: What future cost follows if I accept this? Some imperfections are static, others compound.

That is the right logic for a marriage decision, a surgery decision, a visible dental result, a hire, a major purchase, a contract, a launch. And here too the framework shifts what counts as rational attention. It is often a mistake to obsess over small imperfections that have low salience, low functional impact, and low downstream consequence. But it is also a mistake to wave away a flaw that sits in a highly irreversible and functionally central part of the outcome. So the one-off threshold framework is less about pattern recognition and more about acceptability under consequence. Not “is this fluctuating?” but “can I live with this as the thing that now exists?”

The more interesting thing, though, is not just that there are two kinds of judgment, but that life is largely made of transitions between them. A process ripens into a threshold, and then that threshold creates a new process. Courtship becomes marriage. Interviewing becomes hiring. Symptoms become surgery. Product iteration becomes launch. Then after the threshold, a new reality starts to unfold over time: married life, employee performance, recovery, post-launch behavior. So the real rhythm is process, threshold, regime. Or if you want it even tighter: observation, commitment, consequence. And each phase asks for a different kind of intelligence. In the process phase, you are trying not to mistake noise for signal. At the threshold, you are trying not to evade commitment by hiding inside further observation. In the regime phase, you are trying to see what the decision has actually initialized rather than continuing to judge it by the romance or anxiety that surrounded the threshold itself.

This also means that the transition from process to threshold is not arbitrary. A process should culminate in a decision when the signal is strong enough, stable enough, and persistent enough that further waiting adds less clarity than it adds cost. And the transition from threshold to regime should also be made explicit: once the outcome is accepted, the question is no longer whether the decision felt right in the abstract, but what sort of ongoing world it has created. A lot of people keep using pre-decision language long after the threshold has been crossed. They continue to narrate instead of monitor. But once the threshold has been crossed, what matters is the quality of the regime it initialized.

This became concrete enough that I mocked up a small web prototype around it. No backend, no grand theory engine, just a page that asks first what kind of judgment you are making. Are you observing a process, deciding on a threshold, checking whether a process is ripe for a decision, or evaluating the regime created by a past decision? Only after that do you score the relevant axes, not with fake certainty, but with estimates, confidence, and importance. The point is not to get a magical number. The point is to force the right ontology before forcing a verdict. The page then returns something like healthy and stable, mixed but legible, too early to judge, ready for threshold decision, accept, accept with monitoring, rework, drifting regime, and so on. Prototype: [tyemirov.net/timeseries](https://tyemirov.net/timeseries/). Example scenarios: [tyemirov.net/timeseries](https://tyemirov.net/timeseries/).

What I like about this way of thinking is that it cuts against two opposite pathologies. One is melodrama: treating every local deviation as revelation. One bad date, one bad grade, one bad week, one argument, one bad quarter, and suddenly people talk as if the truth has been disclosed. The other is drift: refusing to recognize that a threshold has arrived and that continued observation is now just a way of avoiding responsibility. That happens in relationships, in careers, in business, in health, everywhere. So the practical question is often not “what do I think about this?” but “what kind of thing is this?” Am I watching a process? Crossing a threshold? Or living inside a regime created by a past threshold? That seems like a much cleaner place to start.
